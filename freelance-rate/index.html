Guide

How to use it

Enter the income you want to keep, your yearly business costs, an estimated tax rate and how many hours you can actually bill. We work back to the hourly, day, weekly and project rates that cover it all.

Steps

  1. Enter the yearly income you want to take home and your yearly business expenses.
  2. Set your estimated tax rate, working weeks, hours per week and the share of hours you can bill.
  3. Read your minimum hourly, day and project rates, then round up when you quote.

The rate formula

Gross the income you want to keep by dividing by 1 minus your tax reserve. Add yearly business expenses. Multiply by 1 plus the profit buffer. Divide by billable hours, which are working weeks times hours per week times the billable share. Day rate is hourly times a full day. A project price is hourly times the hours you will actually spend, including revisions.

Worked example using the defaults

$60,000 take-home at a 25 percent reserve is $80,000 before tax. Plus $10,000 of costs is $90,000. A 10 percent buffer makes $99,000 of revenue to bill. 48 weeks times 40 hours times 70 percent is 1,344 billable hours. The floor is $73.66 an hour, about $589 a day, and about $1,473 for a 20-hour project before you round up. Quoting $75 or $80 leaves room for scope you did not write down.

Tax reserve versus a W-2 calculator

The percent is a planning reserve. On $80,000 of self-employment profit, self-employment tax alone is about 15.3 percent of 92.35 percent, or about $11,304, before income tax and before the deduction for half of that tax. The wage calculator does not compute that. Owner draws are not an expense. Typing 100 percent billable underprices the week you spend on proposals. Use the invoice generator to bill the rate you settled, and the budget calculator to park the tax reserve as a need.

Frequently asked questions

Walk through the default rate: $60,000 take-home, 25 percent tax, $10,000 costs.

First gross the take-home up for tax: $60,000 divided by 1 minus 0.25 = $80,000 you must bill before tax. Add $10,000 of business expenses to get $90,000. A 10 percent profit buffer multiplies that by 1.10, so you need $99,000 of revenue. Working 48 weeks at 40 hours is 1,920 hours. At a 70 percent billable share you can invoice 1,344 hours. $99,000 divided by 1,344 is $73.66 an hour. An 8-hour day is about $589. A 20-hour project is about $1,473 before you round. Round up to a number you will actually quote, such as $75 or $80, because scope grows and some hours will not be billed.

What does the 25 percent tax box represent, and what does it miss?

It is a planning reserve, not a filed return. US self-employed people generally pay self-employment tax of 15.3 percent on 92.35 percent of net profit, plus federal income tax and often state income tax. On $80,000 of net profit, self-employment tax alone is about $11,304 before the deduction for half of it. A 25 to 35 percent combined reserve is a common planning range, but a high-earning filer in a high-tax state can need more, and a low-profit year can need less. The box does not compute the qualified business income deduction, the home-office deduction, or quarterly vouchers. Revisit it after you see last year's effective rate, and use the 2026 wage calculator only for W-2 pay, not for this profit.

Why is 100 percent of my week not billable?

Proposals, email, invoicing, bookkeeping, and learning are real work and they are usually not on the invoice. 60 to 75 percent billable is a realistic band for an independent who also sells the work. 70 percent of a 40-hour week is 28 billable hours. If you type 100 percent, the hourly rate looks comfortingly low and you will miss the income target as soon as a week includes a sales call. Time off is handled by working weeks: 48 weeks leaves about four weeks for holidays, illness, and a short break. If you take eight weeks off, drop working weeks to 44 and let the rate rise.

How do I turn the hourly rate into a project quote?

Estimate the hours honestly, including revisions and project management, then multiply by the hourly rate. A job you think is 20 hours at $73.66 is about $1,473, and a 15 percent revision buffer makes it about $1,694. Fixed bids should use the buffered number, not the best case. Day rates assume a full day; if the client only books two hours, the day rate divided by eight underprices the context-switching. The tool shows hourly, day, weekly, and project figures from the same revenue target so you do not invent a second rate.

What expenses belong in the yearly cost box?

Software, insurance, equipment you will replace this year, contractor help, accounting, and a modest marketing budget. Do not put your personal rent in the box unless a real portion is a deductible office you would not pay otherwise; mixing household costs into the rate is fine for personal planning but label it so you do not treat it as a business deduction. Owner draws are not an expense. The income box is what you want to keep after tax, so do not also add your draw as a cost or you will double count it.

Is this rate stored anywhere?

No. Income, costs, tax percent, and hours stay in the browser. Export PDF, Excel, or CSV, or send a note to your phone, from the page itself. There is no profile and no marketplace listing. The rate is a planning figure, not a contract and not tax advice.
